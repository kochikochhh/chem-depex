The four factors that decide SN1, SN2, E1 or E2

Every substitution and elimination problem is answered by checking the same four things, in this order. Your guide spreads them over pp. 123–131; Klein condenses them into one chart (Fig. 8.31, p. 389).

Must know

  • Reagent: is it a strong nucleophile, a strong base, both, or neither?
  • Substrate: 1° favors SN2; 3° favors SN1/E1 or E2; 2° can go any way.
  • Leaving group: good leaving groups are weak bases (I⁻, Br⁻, TsO⁻, H₂O). –OH and –OR must be protonated first.
  • Solvent: polar protic (water, alcohols) helps SN1; polar aprotic (DMSO, DMF, acetone) helps SN2.
  • Heat pushes toward elimination.

Strong nucleophile and strong baseHO⁻, CH₃O⁻, C₂H₅O⁻ (NaOH, NaOMe, NaOEt)SN2 and E2 compete
Bulky strong baset-BuO⁻ (t-BuOK)E2, even on 1°; gives the less substituted alkene

Why the split? Nucleophilicity is about rate of attack on carbon and grows with charge and polarizability (big atoms like S and I). Basicity is about equilibrium with H⁺. H⁻ is a strong base but too small and unpolarizable to be a good nucleophile; I⁻ is the reverse. Klein pp. 383–385, Fig. 8.25

Week 12 · Substitution and elimination of other substrates · guide pp. 136–138, 150

Alcohols, ethers, epoxides and charged substrates

Same four mechanisms, different leaving groups. The trick in every case is how the bad leaving group (–OH, –OR, –NR₃⁺) gets turned into a good one.

Must know

  • –OH is a terrible leaving group (it would leave as OH⁻, a strong base). Protonate it (HX, H₂SO₄), complex it with a Lewis acid (ZnCl₂), or convert it (TsCl, SOCl₂, PBr₃).
  • ROH + HX: 3°, allylic, benzylic by SN1; methyl and 1° by SN2. HI > HBr > HCl.
  • Dehydration (conc. H₂SO₄, heat): E1 for 2° and 3°, gives the Zaitsev alkene, and the cation can rearrange.
  • Ethers survive base but are cleaved by hot HI or HBr.
  • Charged substrates (R–NR₃⁺, R–SR₂⁺) eliminate to the least substituted alkene (Hofmann).

Ethers: making and breaking them

Put the bulky part on the alkoxide. Ethyl isopropyl ether: sodium isopropoxide + ethyl chloride works; sodium ethoxide + isopropyl chloride mostly gives propene by E2.

Ethers are not cleaved by SOCl₂ or PCl₃ (guide p. 138). An aryl ether (Ar–O–R) gives phenol + R–X, because the ring carbon can't do SN1 or SN2. Klein p. 645

Charged substrates: Hofmann elimination

CH₃CH₂CH(N⁺(CH₃)₃)CH₃  OH⁻, heat ──E2──▶ CH₃CH₂CH=CH₂ (major, 95%) + CH₃CH=CHCH₃ (5%)

Quaternary ammonium and sulfonium salts give mainly the least substituted alkene (Hofmann rule), the opposite of Zaitsev. The big, charged leaving group and steric crowding make the base take the most accessible β-H. Compare 2-bromobutane + KOEt, which gives mainly 2-butene. guide p. 145 · Klein 23.9, pp. 1125–1127

Week 12 · Nucleophilic substitution at saturated carbons · guide pp. 122–140

Nucleophilic substitution at saturated carbon

A nucleophile replaces a leaving group on an sp3 carbon of an alkyl halide, alcohol or ether.

Must know

  • SN2: one step, rate = k[RX][Nu], backside attack, inversion. CH₃ > 1° > 2°; never 3°. Needs a strong Nu; polar aprotic solvent helps.
  • SN1: two steps through a carbocation, rate = k[RX], racemization, rearrangements possible. 3° > 2°; allylic and benzylic too. Weak Nu, polar protic solvent.
  • Nucleophile strength: anion > neutral; bigger atom more polarizable; in protic solvents I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻ (reversed in aprotic).
  • Leaving group: the weaker the base, the better. TsO⁻ > I⁻ > Br⁻ ≈ H₂O > Cl⁻ » F⁻; OH⁻, RO⁻, H⁻, R⁻ don't leave.
  • Solvolysis looks first-order either way, so kinetics alone can't identify the mechanism.

Players

Nu:⁻  +  R–L  ──▶  Nu–R  +  :L⁻
nucleophile  substrate  product  leaving group
  • Nucleophile: an anion or a neutral species with an unshared electron pair to form a new bond to carbon.
  • Reactive site: a saturated C attached to L, which is usually more electronegative than C, so the C is δ+.
  • Leaving group: leaves with the bonding pair (heterolytic cleavage), so it is itself a nucleophile.

The experimental facts, and the two mechanisms they point to

1° R–L → SN2

Rate ∝ [R–L][Nu−]. Product is inverted.

Nu⁻ + C–L ──▶ [Nu···C···L]‡ ──▶ Nu–C + L⁻
one concerted step; backside attack
  • The TS involves two species: bimolecular, second order.
  • L is still attached when Nu arrives, so Nu must come from the back. This gives Walden inversion.

3° R–L → SN1

Rate ∝ [R–L], independent of Nu−. Product is partly racemized, with slightly more inversion.

step 1 (slow):  R–L ──▶ R⁺ + L⁻   (ionization)
step 2 (fast):  R⁺ + Nu⁻ ──▶ R–Nu
  • Only one species in the slow step: unimolecular, first order.
  • The carbocation is planar, so Nu can attack either face.

For 2° R–L, kinetics and stereochemistry are not fixed; either pathway can operate depending on conditions.

Factor A: substrate structure

SN1 depends on carbocation stability

3° » 2° > 1° > CH₃

3° cations are stabilized by C–H hyperconjugation, the +I effect of R groups, and steric acceleration (going from sp3 to sp2 relieves crowding). 1° and methyl substrates do not react by SN1 unless R is allyl, propargyl or benzyl, whose cations are resonance-stabilized.

SN2 depends on steric hindrance

CH₃ > 1° > 2° > 3°

In the TS the carbon carries five groups and is crowded. SN2 is not known to occur at 3° carbons.

Factor B: the nucleophile

Basicity is about an equilibrium with a proton. Nucleophilicity is about the rate of reaction with carbon. Nu strength matters only for SN2, since in SN1 Nu is not in the slow step.

  1. Charge: an anion beats its conjugate acid. OH− > H2O; RO− > ROH.
  2. Basicity: with the same nucleophilic atom, nucleophilicity parallels basicity: RO− > HO− > RCOO− > ROH > H2O.
  3. Polarizability: bigger atoms hold outer electrons more loosely, so they are better nucleophiles.
  4. Solvation: protic solvents H-bond to anions and weaken them. In protic solvents I− > Br− > Cl− > F− (F− is most solvated). In aprotic solvents the order reverses: F− > Cl− > Br− > I−.
  5. Size and shape: bulky nucleophiles are slowed in SN2.

SN1 typically uses weak neutral nucleophiles (H2O, ROH). SN2 needs strong ones (OH−, RO−, R3N).

Catalysis

  • Iodide catalysis: I− is both a strong nucleophile and a good leaving group. Adding KI to R–CH2Cl + H2O first makes R–CH2I, which water then attacks quickly.
  • Electrophilic catalysis: H+ or a Lewis acid (BX3, AlX3, ZnCl2) complexes the O of an alcohol or ether so it leaves as H2O or ROH. Ag+ pulls X− off R–X by precipitating AgX.

Factor D: the solvent

Polarity (dielectric constant) helps reactions that create or separate charge. SN1 forms ions in its slow step, so it speeds up a lot in polar solvents. Water (80) is the best ionizing solvent, but organic compounds dissolve poorly in it, so mixed solvents like EtOH–H2O are used; more water means more polar.

Rule: making ions from neutral molecules is accelerated by polar solvents; making neutral molecules from ions is slowed.

Protic vs aprotic: protic solvents (H2O, ROH) H-bond to the nucleophile, which shields it and slows SN2. Polar aprotic solvents (DMF, DMSO, DMA, HMPT) solvate cations but leave the anion free. CH3I + NaN3 runs about 100,000 times faster in DMF than in methanol, even though their polarities are similar.

Solvolysis

The solvent is the nucleophile: hydrolysis (water), methanolysis (methanol). Solvents are weak nucleophiles, so solvolysis usually goes by SN1, but with 1° substrates the large excess of solvent can push SN2. Because [solvent] stays constant, the kinetics look first-order either way (pseudo first-order: rate = k[solvent][RL] = k′[RL]), so kinetics alone cannot tell you the mechanism.

Stereochemistry, cyclization and rearrangement

  • (S)-2-bromobutane + OH− under SN2 conditions → (R)-2-butanol (inverted).
  • Under SN1 conditions → a mixture of (R)- and (S)-2-butanol (racemization).
  • Cyclization is an intramolecular SN: it happens when a second group in the molecule has a lone pair and sits 3–5 atoms from the reacting carbon.
  • Carbocation rearrangement (1,2-shift): an H (hydride shift), alkyl (methyl shift) or aryl group moves with its electron pair from the neighboring carbon if this makes a more stable cation.

Functional groups that undergo SN

Alkyl halides

Reactivity R–I > R–Br > R–Cl > R–F. Methyl, 1° and 2° halides go SN2 with good nucleophiles (OH−, OR−, CN−). 3° halides go SN1 with H2O, ROH. Allylic and benzylic halides react quickly by either path.

Alcohols

Substitute only in acid, because –OH must become –OH2+ to leave. Reactivity toward HX: benzylic, allylic > 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl. HX reactivity: HI > HBr > HCl > HF. All alcohols react with conc. HBr or HI. With conc. HCl, only 3°, benzyl and allyl alcohols react readily; 1° and 2° need anhydrous ZnCl2 and heat (the Lucas test). 2°, 3°, allylic, benzylic → SN1; methyl and 1° → SN2.

Ethers

Cleaved by HI or HBr (not by SOCl2 or PCl3). CH3–O–CH2CH2CH3 + HI → CH3I + propanol (SN2 at the less hindered methyl). (CH3)3C–OCH3 + HI → (CH3)3C–I + CH3OH (SN1 via the 3° cation).

Williamson ether synthesis

M⁺ ⁻OR  +  R′–L  ──SN2──▶  R–O–R′  +  M⁺L⁻
alkoxide: R = CH₃, 1°, 2°, 3°, Ar     halide: R′ = CH₃ or 1° (L = –X, –OSO₂OR)

Put the bulky part on the alkoxide and keep the halide methyl or primary. A 2° or 3° halide with a strong-base alkoxide gives elimination instead. Example: ethyl isopropyl ether is best made from sodium isopropoxide + ethyl chloride. Phenoxide + dimethyl sulfate gives anisole.

Details that earn the extra point

  • Steric hindrance one carbon over still counts: neopentyl bromide is primary, but its bulky neighbor makes SN2 extremely slow. p. 300
  • Walden inversion is the name for the SN2 flip; draw the nucleophile coming in 180° from the leaving group. p. 297
  • Polar aprotic solvents to memorize: DMSO, DMF, acetone, acetonitrile (also HMPA). They wrap the cation and leave the anion "naked". pp. 324–325
  • Turning OH into a leaving group without acid: convert to a tosylate (TsCl, pyridine), then do SN2. The tosylation step keeps the configuration. pp. 324, 328
  • Klein's three-question method for "which mechanism?": substrate, nucleophile strength, then leaving group and solvent. 7.8, pp. 320–326
Week 12 · Elimination reactions · guide pp. 133, 141–152

Elimination reactions

Elimination always competes with substitution. Instead of attacking carbon, the base removes a β-H, and a C=C forms as the leaving group departs. No β-H means no elimination.

Must know

  • E2: one step, rate = k[RX][base], H and L anti-periplanar (both axial in a cyclohexane).
  • E1: carbocation first, rate = k[RX]; 3° with weak base, often alongside SN1; can rearrange.
  • E beats S with: strong or bulky base, crowded substrate, less polar solvent, heat, EWG on the β-carbon.
  • Zaitsev: neutral substrates give the more substituted alkene. Hofmann: charged substrates (R–NR₃⁺) or bulky bases give the less substituted one.
  • The trans (E) alkene is usually favored.

The two mechanisms

E2: one step, bimolecular

B:⁻ + H–Cβ–Cα–L ──▶ C=C + B–H + L⁻
rate = k[substrate][base]

The base pulls off the β-H at the same time as L leaves from the neighboring carbon.

E1: two steps, unimolecular

1. (CH₃)₃C–Br ──slow──▶ (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Br⁻
2. base removes β-H ──▶ (CH₃)₂C=CH₂
rate = k[substrate]

Seen with 3° (sometimes 2°) substrates, weak or dilute base, and conditions that favor carbocations, such as solvolysis. Rearrangements are possible.

A stronger or more concentrated base shifts things toward E2, for 1° and 3° substrates alike. Pulling off an outer H is little affected by crowding, unlike attack at a hindered carbon.

Elimination vs substitution

Substitution needs less bond reorganization and usually wins, but these factors shift the balance toward elimination:

  1. Strong base. E needs a strong base; S needs a good nucleophile. 2-Bromopropane with NaOEt/EtOH gives 79% propene and 21% ether. In plain EtOH, the weakly basic solvent gives 3% alkene and 97% ether. RS− is a good nucleophile but a weak base, so it gives mainly substitution.
  2. Crowded substrate (and bulky base). Crowding is relieved when groups leave, the β-H is easier to reach than the crowded carbon, and more substituted substrates give more alkylated, more stable alkenes.
  3. Less polar solvent. The E transition state spreads the charge over more atoms (5 vs 3), so it is favored by lower solvent polarity.
  4. Higher temperature. 2-Bromopropane + NaOH in EtOH/H2O: 53% E at 45 °C, 64% E at 100 °C. t-Butyl chloride: 17% E at 25 °C, 64% E at 65 °C.
  5. Electron-withdrawing group on the β-carbon makes the β-H more acidic.

Direction of elimination (regioselectivity)

  • Saytzeff (Zaitsev) rule: neutral substrates give mainly the more substituted alkene. The preference is moderate and stronger in E1 than in E2. 2-Bromobutane + KOEt gives mainly 2-butene.
  • Hofmann rule (anti-Saytzeff): charged substrates (quaternary ammonium, sulfonium salts) give mainly the less substituted alkene, strongly, usually by E2. Bulky bases such as t-BuOK also push toward the less substituted alkene.

Contributing factors: stability of the developing alkene, steric effects in the substrate and the base at the transition state, solvation, and base–leaving-group interaction.

Stereochemistry of elimination

The C–H and C–L bonds must lie in one plane for the new π bond to form. They can be syn-periplanar (same side, eclipsed) or anti-periplanar (opposite sides, staggered). Anti elimination is the usual course, because the staggered arrangement is lower in energy. Elimination is stereoselective: the isomer with the bulkiest groups on opposite sides (E/trans) usually predominates.

  • If there is only one β-H, E2 gives only the one alkene that anti elimination allows. (2S,3R)-2-chloro-3-pentanol gives (E)-2-penten-3-ol; (2S,3S) gives the (Z) isomer.
  • E1 gives both E and Z, with E (bulky groups apart) as the major product.
  • Cyclohexanes: L and the β-H must both be axial (trans-diaxial). A conformer with a bulky group forced axial is rare, so a compound whose stable conformer has L equatorial reacts slowly.

Common eliminations

Dehydrohalogenation:  CH₃CHClCH₂CH₃ + KOEt/EtOH ──▶ CH₃CH=CHCH₃ (major) + CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃
Dehydration:          RCH₂CH₂OH ──H₂SO₄, heat──▶ RCH=CH₂ + H₂O   (3° > 2° > 1°)
Exercise IV.7: A strong base like NaOH or NaOEt favors E2 over E1. 2-Bromobutane gives (E)- and (Z)-2-butene. Ethyl isopropyl ether should be made from sodium isopropoxide + ethyl chloride, because sodium ethoxide + isopropyl chloride mainly gives E2.

Regio and stereo rules for eliminations

  • Base size picks the alkene. A small base like NaOEt gives mostly the Zaitsev (more substituted) alkene; bulky t-BuOK gives mostly the Hofmann (less substituted) one. pp. 361–363
  • Alkene stability: more substituted is more stable, and trans is more stable than cis. That's why Zaitsev and trans products dominate. 8.5, p. 353
  • E2 is stereospecific: when the β-carbon has only one H, anti-periplanar geometry forces a single alkene isomer. Draw a Newman projection with H and L anti to find it. 8.7, pp. 358–371
  • Cyclohexanes: L must be axial. If the ring's stable chair puts L equatorial, E2 is slow and may give an unexpected (non-Zaitsev) alkene. 8.7, pp. 358–371
  • Don't expect one winner. Klein's advice: list every product and label major and minor. 2° + NaOEt gives E2 major, SN2 minor. p. 383
  • One-glance summary: Table 8.2 (regio and stereo for all four mechanisms, p. 391) and Fig. 8.31 (master chart, p. 389).

Nucleophilic addition to aldehydes and ketones

The carbonyl carbon is δ+ and attracts nucleophiles. Aldehydes and ketones add rather than substitute because their would-be leaving groups, H− and R−, are extremely poor.

Must know

  • Aldehydes and ketones add rather than substitute, because H⁻ and R⁻ can't leave.
  • Reactivity: HCHO > RCHO > R₂CO (alkyl groups donate electrons and add bulk).
  • Strong-base nucleophiles (H⁻, R⁻, CN⁻) add directly; weak ones (H₂O, ROH, RNH₂) need an acid catalyst. Most additions are reversible.
  • Know the products: cyanohydrin, hemiacetal → acetal, imine/oxime/hydrazone, aldol, Cannizzaro, iodoform.
  • Grignard: HCHO → 1° alcohol, RCHO → 2°, ketone → 3°.

What makes C=O reactive

  1. Electrophilic carbonyl C: open to nucleophilic attack.
  2. Nucleophilic carbonyl O: open to electrophilic attack, usually by H+.
  3. Acidic α-H: the α-C can become carbanionic, leading to enols and enolates.
  4. sp2 carbonyl C can share π delocalization with neighboring groups, which changes reactivity.
R–C(=O)–H(R) + H–Nu ──▶ R–C(OH)(Nu)–H(R)     (the adduct)

Acid- vs base-catalyzed addition

A · Acid-catalyzed (weak Nu–H)

1. C=O + H⁺ ──▶ C=OH⁺   (C more positive)
2. Nu–H attacks C, then loses H⁺

B · Base-catalyzed (strong Nu⁻)

1. Nu⁻ attacks C ──▶ alkoxide C–O⁻
2. O⁻ takes H⁺ from H–Nu or water

The steps are the same, in reverse order. These additions are reversible, so the outcome depends on the equilibrium (Keq). SN reactions are essentially irreversible and depend on rates.

Factors in carbonyl reactivity

  1. Resonance with adjacent π electrons (as in aryl ketones) stabilizes C=O and lowers reactivity.
  2. Inductive effects: −I groups (e.g. Cl on the α-C) make C more positive and more reactive. +I groups (alkyls) lower reactivity.
  3. Steric effects: bulky groups crowd the tetrahedral adduct and lower reactivity.
Aldehydes are more reactive than ketones: the second R on a ketone donates electrons (+I) and is bulkier than H. Order: HCHO > RCHO > R2CO, with aryl carbonyls lower still.

A. Simple addition

1 · HCN: cyanohydrins

R–CO–H(R) + HCN ──(CN⁻ catalyst)──▶ R–C(OH)(CN)–H(R)   cyanohydrin
   ├─ H₃O⁺ ──▶ α-hydroxy acid  R–C(OH)(COOH)
   └─ reduction ──▶ β-amino alcohol  R–C(OH)–CH₂NH₂

HCN (a very toxic gas) is a poor nucleophile and adds slowly. KCN or a base supplies CN−, which attacks first (mechanism B). A new C–C bond forms and the product has two functional groups.

2 · Sodium bisulfite

C=O + NaHSO3 → crystalline bisulfite adduct. This is used to separate aldehydes and ketones from mixtures. Most aldehydes give 70–90%; methyl ketones only 12–56% because of steric hindrance.

3 · Grignard reagents and hydrides

R′MgX adds R′− to the carbonyl C; after H3O+ workup: HCHO → 1° alcohol, RCHO → 2° alcohol, ketone → 3° alcohol. NaBH4 or LiAlH4 adds H−: aldehyde → 1° alcohol, ketone → 2° alcohol (butanone + NaBH4 → 2-butanol).

4 · α,β-Unsaturated aldehydes and ketones

C=C–C=O + Nu–H ──▶ C=C–C(OH)Nu      direct (1,2) addition
                ──▶ Nu–C–CH–C=O      conjugate (1,4) addition

Direct addition with strongly basic nucleophiles: RLi, RMgX, LiAlH4. Conjugate addition with weakly basic ones: CN−, amines, RSH, X−.

B. Addition followed by elimination

1 · Alcohols: hemiacetals and acetals

aldehyde + R′OH ⇌ hemiacetal ──+R′OH, H⁺, −H₂O──▶ acetal
ketone   + R′OH ⇌ hemiketal  ──+R′OH, H⁺, −H₂O──▶ ketal

Acid catalysis: protonate C=O, add one ROH (hemiacetal), protonate –OH and lose water (giving a resonance-stabilized cation), add the second ROH (acetal). Base can catalyze only the hemiacetal step, because –OH is a poor leaving group. Open-chain hemiacetals are usually unfavorable (Keq < 1), but cyclic ones are important: glucose is a cyclic hemiacetal and fructose a cyclic hemiketal.

2 · Aldol condensation

2 CH₃CHO ──10% NaOH──▶ CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO   3-hydroxybutanal (an aldol)

1. OH⁻ removes an α-H ──▶ enolate ion (the nucleophile)
2. enolate adds to the C=O of a second molecule ──▶ alkoxide
3. alkoxide takes H⁺ from water ──▶ aldol, OH⁻ regenerated

The aldehyde must have an α-H. Two different aldehydes give a crossed aldol. Ketones (e.g. acetone) give aldols too, but the equilibrium is unfavorable. The aldol links two small molecules with a new C–C bond.

3 · Cannizzaro reaction

An aldehyde with no α-H, heated with concentrated base (50% KOH), disproportionates: half is oxidized to the carboxylate, half reduced to the alcohol. 2 HCHO → HCOO− + CH3OH. 2 C6H5CHO → C6H5COO− + C6H5CH2OH. Mechanism: OH− adds to C=O, then the adduct transfers a hydride to a second aldehyde.

4 · α-Halogenation

Ketones are halogenated at the α-carbon in base (via the enolate; enolate formation is slow) or in acid (via the enol; enol formation is the slow step).

5 · Haloform (iodoform) test

R–CO–CH₃ + 3 I₂ + 4 OH⁻ ──▶ R–COO⁻ + CHI₃↓ (yellow solid) + 3 I⁻ + 3 H₂O

Positive for methyl ketones, acetaldehyde, and alcohols that oxidize to them (R–CH(OH)–CH3, ethanol). The CH3 is triply iodinated, then CI3− is pushed out.

6 · Derivatives of ammonia (H2N–Z)

C=O + H₂N–Z ──▶ C=N–Z + H₂O     (add, then eliminate water; faster in mild acid)
Reagent H2N–ZProduct
hydroxylamine, H2N–OHoxime
hydrazine, H2N–NH2hydrazone

Application: amino acid synthesis

Strecker synthesis: aldehyde + NH3 + HCN → α-aminonitrile; hydrolysis → amino acid. Acetaldehyde gives alanine (60%). Alternatively, an α-halo acid + excess NH3 gives the amino acid.

Patterns that make the reactions predictable

  • Strong vs weak nucleophile rule: H⁻ (LiAlH₄, NaBH₄) and C⁻ (RMgX) add irreversibly in basic conditions. Weak ones (H₂O, ROH, amines) add reversibly and need acid. 20.4, pp. 937–938

  • 1° amine → imine; 2° amine → enamine. Imine formation is fastest in mildly acidic solution (around pH 4–5). pp. 947–955
  • Wolff–Kishner (H₂NNH₂, KOH, heat) turns C=O into CH₂ through a hydrazone, the basic partner of the Clemmensen reduction. p. 951
  • Cannizzaro is a hydride-transfer reaction; Klein files it under hydrogen nucleophiles. p. 961
  • Grignard limits: no –OH, –NH, –COOH or water anywhere in the molecule, because the Grignard grabs the proton first. pp. 597–598
  • Aldol condensation: heating the aldol loses water and gives a conjugated α,β-unsaturated carbonyl. p. 1059
  • Haloform works because CX₃⁻ is stabilized enough to leave. pp. 1055–1056

Nucleophilic acyl substitution

The carboxylic acid family, R–CO–L with L = –Cl, –OCOR, –OR, –OH, –NH2, has a carbonyl carbon bonded to two electronegative atoms. Unlike aldehydes and ketones, these compounds have a leaving group, so the tetrahedral adduct collapses and expels L.

Must know

  • R–CO–L: the nucleophile adds, then L leaves (addition–elimination) through a tetrahedral intermediate.
  • Reactivity follows leaving-group ability: acid chloride > anhydride > ester > acid > amide.
  • You can only go "downhill": a more reactive derivative makes a less reactive one.
  • Acid-catalyzed steps protonate C=O first; base steps attack first.
  • Saponification goes to completion because the carboxylate can't react back.

Addition, then elimination

R–CO–L + Nu⁻ ──addition──▶ R–C(O⁻)(L)(Nu) ──elimination──▶ R–CO–Nu + L⁻
                        tetrahedral intermediate

Aldehydes and ketones stop at addition because –H and –R are extremely strong bases and very poor leaving groups.

Leaving-group ability sets reactivity

Leaving ability:  Cl⁻ > RCOO⁻ > RO⁻ > OH⁻ > NH₂⁻

Reactivity:  acid chloride > anhydride > ester > carboxylic acid > amide

Good leaving groups (Cl−, RCOO−) leave directly. –OR and –OH become good leaving groups when protonated (acid conditions). –NH2 is not normally a leaving group, which is why amides are least reactive.

Reactive derivatives (acid chlorides)

1. Nu⁻ attacks C=O
2. Cl⁻ leaves, C=O re-forms

Less reactive ones (acid catalysis)

1. protonate carbonyl O
2. Nu attacks the activated C
3. proton transfer
4. L leaves as a neutral molecule
5. lose H⁺ ──▶ product

Interconversions

A more reactive derivative can easily be turned into a less reactive one (downhill), not the reverse.

CH₃COOH + SOCl₂ ──▶ CH₃COCl + SO₂ + HCl                       (acid → acid chloride)
CH₃COCl + CH₃OH ──▶ CH₃COOCH₃ + HCl                           (esterification)
CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ + CH₃OH ──H⁺──▶ CH₃COOCH₃ + CH₃CH₂OH            (transesterification)
CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ + H₂O ──H⁺──▶ CH₃CH₂COOH + CH₃OH               (acid hydrolysis)
CH₃CH₂COOCH₃ + NaOH ──▶ CH₃CH₂COO⁻Na⁺ + CH₃OH               (saponification)
2 CH₃COOH ──AlPO₄, heat──▶ (CH₃CO)₂O + H₂O                    (acetic anhydride)
succinic acid ──heat──▶ succinic anhydride + H₂O
RCOCl + 2 NH₃ ──▶ RCONH₂ + NH₄Cl                              (amide)

Saponification goes to completion because the acid formed is a stronger acid than the alcohol, so it is deprotonated to the carboxylate and cannot react back.

Nitrile hydrolysis and α-halogenation

  • Nitriles: R–C≡N + H2O (H+ or OH−) → amide → R–COOH (+ NH4+ or NH3).
  • Acid halides can be halogenated at the α-C with X2 and H+ or OH−, through the enol, just like ketones: CH3COCl + Cl2 → ClCH2COCl.

Nucleophilic aromatic substitution

The flip side of EAS: here a nucleophile attacks the ring. Aryl halides (Ar–X) barely react under conditions where alkyl halides react easily; chlorobenzene refluxed with NaOH gives no substitution.

Must know

  • Why Ar–X resists SN1/SN2: no backside attack, unstable phenyl cation, stronger C–X bond.
  • Addition–elimination: needs EWGs (–NO₂) ortho or para to X; goes through the Meisenheimer carbanion. More EWGs, milder conditions.
  • Elimination–addition: no EWG, very strong base (NaNH₂/KNH₂) → benzyne; can give more than one product.
  • Diazonium salts (ArNH₂ + NaNO₂/HCl, 0–5 °C) are replaced by Cl, Br, CN (Sandmeyer), F, I, OH, H; they also couple with activated rings to give azo dyes.

Why aryl halides resist SN1 and SN2

  1. The ring blocks backside attack, so SN2 cannot happen.
  2. Phenyl cations are very unstable, so SN1 does not happen.
  3. The C–X bond is shorter and stronger, with partial double-bond character from π delocalization. Breaking it needs more energy either way.

Under the right conditions it does work, and electron-withdrawing groups make it much easier:

Aryl chloride + OH−Conditions needed
chlorobenzeneNaOH, 350 °C, high pressure, then H+
p-nitrochlorobenzeneaq. NaHCO3, 130 °C
2,4-dinitrochlorobenzeneaq. NaHCO3, 100 °C

A. Addition–elimination (activated aryl halides)

Applies when a strongly electron-withdrawing group is ortho or para to –X. The more such groups, the faster the reaction and the milder the conditions.

Ar(NO₂)–Cl + OH⁻ ──addition (slow)──▶ [Meisenheimer complex]⁻ ──elimination (fast)──▶ Ar(NO₂)–OH + Cl⁻
                                       delocalized carbanion
Key point: the negative charge of the Meisenheimer complex sits on the carbons ortho and para to the attacked carbon. A nitro group there can take the charge onto its oxygens by resonance. A meta nitro group cannot, so it helps much less.

B. Elimination–addition (benzyne)

Applies to unactivated aryl halides. It needs forcing conditions (high T and P) or a very strong base such as KNH2 in liquid NH3.

C₆H₅Br + K⁺NH₂⁻ ──elimination (−HBr)──▶ benzyne ──addition of NH₃──▶ C₆H₅NH₂

The "triple bond" of benzyne is not like an alkyne's. One of its bonds comes from sideways overlap of two sp2 orbitals that lie in the plane of the ring, so it is weak and very reactive. The nucleophile can add to either end of the benzyne, so substituted aryl halides can give more than one product.

Exercise IV.5 #2: m-bromoethylbenzene + NaNH2 can form two different benzynes and gives three products (o-, m- and p-ethylaniline). The ortho isomer gives two (o- and m-), and the para isomer gives two (p- and m-).

C. Diazonium ions (aryl cation pathway)

This route makes nucleophilic substitution on the ring easy. First the aromatic amine is converted to a diazonium salt (diazotization), kept cold:

NaNO₂ + HX ──▶ HNO₂ (HONO) + NaX
HONO + H⁺ ──▶ H₂O–NO⁺ ──▶ H₂O + N≡O⁺  (nitrosonium)
R–NH₂ + N≡O⁺ ──▶ R–NH₂⁺–N=O ──▶ R–NH–N=O (N-nitrosamine) ──▶ R–N=N–OH ──H⁺, −H₂O──▶ R–N≡N⁺

Ar–NH₂ + NaNO₂ + HCl (0–5 °C) ──▶ Ar–N₂⁺ Cl⁻   benzenediazonium chloride

N-Nitrosamines are very powerful carcinogens. Most aryl diazonium salts are unstable above 5–10 °C and can explode when dry.

Redox reagents · Lab Activity 10 · guide pp. 174–181

Oxidation and reduction

Redox reactions show up three ways in this chapter: in qualitative tests, in synthesis, and in working out structures.

Must know

  • Oxidation = more bonds to O (or fewer to H); reduction = more bonds to H.
  • 1° alcohol → aldehyde → acid; 2° → ketone; 3° → no reaction.
  • NaBH₄ reduces only aldehydes and ketones; LiAlH₄ also reduces esters, acids, amides and nitriles. Neither touches C=C.
  • Tests: Tollens' (all aldehydes), Benedict's/Fehling's (aliphatic aldehydes), chromic acid (1°/2° alcohols, aldehydes), Baeyer's (C=C).
  • Ozonolysis splits C=C into two C=O, which lets you work backward to the alkene.

  • The same oxidations as the tests: 1° alcohol → aldehyde → acid; 2° alcohol → ketone.
  • Toluene (any alkylbenzene) + hot KMnO4 → benzoic acid.

B. In synthesis: reduction

Catalytic hydrogenation and its relatives

Aldehydes and ketones are reduced by H2/catalyst, NaBH4 or LiAlH4, to 1° and 2° alcohols respectively.

Carboxylic acid derivatives

  • Not reactive enough for catalytic hydrogenation or NaBH4. Only LiAlH4 (a strong hydride source) reduces them: acids and esters → 1° alcohols; amides → amines (R–CONHCH3 → R–CH2NHCH3).
  • Acid chlorides, the most reactive, can be hydrogenated (H2, Pd/C) to the aldehyde and on to the 1° alcohol.
  • Rosenmund reduction: H2 with a deactivated (poisoned) Pd catalyst stops at the aldehyde: CH3CH2COCl → CH3CH2CHO.

Carbonyl to CH2

R–CO–R′ ──Zn(Hg), HCl──▶ R–CH₂–R′          Clemmensen (acidic)
R–CO–R′ ──H₂NNH₂, OH⁻, heat──▶ R–CH₂–R′    Wolff–Kishner (basic)

Nitro to amine

C₆H₅NO₂ ──Sn/HCl──▶ C₆H₅NH₃⁺ ──OH⁻──▶ C₆H₅NH₂ (aniline)

C=C and C≡C carry no partial positive charge, so hydride reagents (NaBH4, LiAlH4) do not reduce them.

acyl chloride > aldehyde > ketone > imine > ester > carboxylic acid > amide > nitrile
(alkenes and alkynes: not reduced by hydride)

C. In structure determination

Ozonolysis (O3, then Zn/H2O)

Cuts each C=C in two and puts =O on both carbons. Doubly bonded C with an H becomes an aldehyde; with two R groups it becomes a ketone; terminal =CH2 becomes formaldehyde.

CH₃CH₂CH=CH₂ ──1) O₃ 2) Zn, H₂O──▶ CH₃CH₂CHO + HCHO
(CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃ ──1) O₃ 2) Zn, H₂O──▶ CH₃COCH₃ + CH₃CHO

With two or more C=C, you get three or more fragments, and the middle one is oxidized at both ends. A ring alkene gives a single chain with C=O at both ends. Alkynes give carboxylic acids.

Working backward: C4H8 that gives only CH3CHO must be CH3CH=CHCH3 (join the two C=O carbons with a double bond). C6H10 that gives one fragment, OHC–CH2CH2CH2CH2–CHO, is cyclohexene.

Hot, concentrated KMnO4

Mild conditions (cold, dilute) give diols. Forcing conditions cleave the C=C: =CH2 → CO2; =CHR → RCOOH; =CR2 → ketone. It often gives mixtures, so ozonolysis is preferred for structure work.

Periodic acid (HIO4)

Cleaves vicinal diols (1,2-diols) into two carbonyl compounds: CH3CH(OH)–CH(OH)CH3 → 2 CH3CHO.

Exercise IV.10 (limonene): C10H16 takes up two equivalents of H2, so it has two C=C (and with three degrees of unsaturation, one ring). Ozonolysis then Zn/acid gives one equivalent of formaldehyde (a terminal =CH2) plus one other carbonyl fragment.

  • Breathalyzer: the orange → green dichromate color change from oxidizing ethanol is the same chemistry as your chromic acid test. p. 610
  • LiAlH₄ vs NaBH₄: LiAlH₄ reacts violently with water, so it's used in dry ether and water is added in a separate second step. NaBH₄ can be used in methanol or water. pp. 590–592
  • Dihydroxylation stereo: cold KMnO₄ or OsO₄ adds both OH on the same face (syn); epoxide then H₃O⁺ gives anti. 9.9–9.10, pp. 440–444
  • Tollens' and Benedict's appear in Klein under carbohydrates: a positive test marks a reducing sugar. p. 1170
